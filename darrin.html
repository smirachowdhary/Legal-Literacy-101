Equal Rights in Darrin v. Gould

A huge part of student life is getting involved with interests through clubs and sports. Around 65% of students in Washington State participate in school athletics. Participating in sports has health benefits, gives a break from academics, and for many athletes, it creates a pathway to college and future careers. Because of this, it is critical to ensure that opportunities are open to everyone, regardless of gender.

Washington State has key legal protections against gender discrimination. Article XXXI (31) of the Washington State Constitution states that “equality of rights and responsibilities under the law shall not be denied or abridged on the account of sex.” Similarly, the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) at the state level prohibits discrimination based on sex. These protections formed the foundation of the landmark case Darrin v. Gould (1975).

The Case

In 1973, sisters Carol and Delores Darrin, students at Wishkah Valley High School, attempted to join their school’s football team. Since no girls’ team existed, they tried out for the boys’ team run by the Washington Interscholastic Activities Association (WIAA). Although they passed all required tests, they were denied solely because they were girls.

Their parents sued the WIAA, arguing that this decision was discriminatory and violated Washington law. The case centered on two key questions: whether the denial was based purely on gender, and if so, whether that constituted a constitutional violation.

After being denied in lower courts, the case reached the Washington Supreme Court. The Court ruled in favor of the Darrin sisters, stating that denying them access based solely on sex violated the state constitution. This decision became one of the first major applications of a state Equal Rights Amendment and set a precedent for gender equality in school athletics.

Background: Equal Rights Amendment

The Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) was a proposed amendment to the U.S. Constitution aimed at guaranteeing equal rights regardless of sex. While it passed Congress, it required ratification by states. Washington played a key role in this movement during the early 1970s.

On March 22, 1973, Washington became the 30th state to pass its version of the ERA. Although the federal amendment was never fully ratified, state-level ERAs—like Washington’s—continue to shape laws and court decisions related to gender equality.
